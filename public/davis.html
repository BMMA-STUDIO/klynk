Miles Davis

1926–1991 | American jazz trumpeter, composer and bandleader

Early Life and Musical Education

Miles Dewey Davis III was born on May 26, 1926, in Alton, Illinois, and grew up in East St. Louis. His father was a dentist and his mother was a music teacher and violinist. Davis received his first trumpet at the age of thirteen and studied under local trumpeter Elwood Buchanan, who encouraged him to develop a clear tone without excessive vibrato.

As a teenager, Davis played with local bands and gained experience performing with touring musicians. In 1944, he moved to New York City to study at the Juilliard School. He also immersed himself in the city's emerging bebop scene, spending time at clubs on 52nd Street and learning directly from leading jazz musicians.

Bebop and Charlie Parker, 1944–1948

Davis soon began performing with saxophonist Charlie Parker, one of the central figures in bebop. He appeared on recordings with Parker and developed his improvisational skills in an environment characterized by rapid tempos, complex harmonies and demanding musical exchanges.

Although Davis learned from Parker, his own trumpet style differed from the more technically aggressive approach associated with some bebop players. He favored space, lyrical phrasing and carefully selected notes. By the late 1940s, he was beginning to establish an identity independent of his mentors.

Birth of the Cool, 1948–1950

Davis assembled a nine-piece ensemble featuring an unusual combination of trumpet, trombone, alto saxophone, baritone saxophone, French horn and tuba alongside a rhythm section. The arrangements, developed with musicians and composers including Gil Evans, Gerry Mulligan and John Lewis, emphasized orchestral color, controlled dynamics and contrapuntal writing.

Recordings made between 1949 and 1950 were subsequently collected as Birth of the Cool. They became foundational works in the development of cool jazz, offering an alternative to the intensity and speed of bebop.

Hard Bop and the Prestige Years, 1950–1955

During the early 1950s, Davis worked with various small groups while recording for Prestige Records. His career was affected by heroin addiction, which damaged his health and professional reliability. He eventually returned to East St. Louis to confront the addiction and rebuild his life and career.

His recordings from this period included sessions with saxophonists Sonny Rollins and Jackie McLean, pianist Horace Silver and other leading musicians. Davis developed a more economical, blues-inflected style that became central to his work as a bandleader.

In 1954, performances and recordings such as Walkin' helped establish the hard bop direction, which combined bebop sophistication with blues, gospel and rhythm-and-blues influences.

Columbia Records and the First Great Quintet, 1955–1958

Davis's performance at the 1955 Newport Jazz Festival helped re-establish him as a major figure in jazz. He signed with Columbia Records and assembled a quintet featuring tenor saxophonist John Coltrane, pianist Red Garland, bassist Paul Chambers and drummer Philly Joe Jones. This group became known for its responsive interplay, blues grounding and inventive improvisation.

Albums such as 'Round About Midnight and Cookin' captured the group at different stages. Davis also continued recording with larger ensembles and collaborators outside the quintet.

In 1957, Davis recorded the soundtrack for Louis Malle's film Ascenseur pour l'échafaud (released internationally as Elevator to the Gallows). The music was largely improvised to accompany scenes from the film and demonstrated his ability to create atmosphere through sparse, expressive trumpet lines.

Orchestral Collaborations with Gil Evans, 1957–1960

Davis reunited with arranger Gil Evans for a series of ambitious recordings that combined jazz improvisation with orchestral writing. Miles Ahead (1957), Porgy and Bess (1958) and Sketches of Spain (1960) explored large-scale arrangements, unusual instrumental combinations and influences from classical and Spanish music.

Rather than functioning as conventional backing, the orchestral arrangements formed an integral part of the musical structure. Davis's trumpet remained the central voice, moving between the ensemble's textures with a controlled and expressive sound.

Kind of Blue and Modal Jazz, 1959

In 1959, Davis recorded Kind of Blue, featuring John Coltrane on tenor saxophone, Cannonball Adderley on alto saxophone, Bill Evans and Wynton Kelly on piano, Paul Chambers on bass and Jimmy Cobb on drums.

The sessions used modal frameworks that gave musicians room to explore scales, melodic development and harmonic color without relying on frequent chord changes. Davis's approach created space for extended improvisation while preserving a strong sense of melodic direction.

Kind of Blue became one of the most influential albums in jazz history. Its spacious sound, disciplined ensemble playing and accessible musical language brought modal jazz to a broad audience and influenced musicians well beyond jazz.

The Second Great Quintet, 1963–1968

By the early 1960s, Davis had begun assembling a new group of younger musicians. The quintet that emerged featured saxophonist Wayne Shorter, pianist Herbie Hancock, bassist Ron Carter and drummer Tony Williams. Together, they developed a highly interactive style that allowed rhythmic, harmonic and formal expectations to shift during performances.

Albums including E.S.P. (1965), Miles Smiles (1967), Sorcerer (1967), Nefertiti (1968) and Filles de Kilimanjaro (1968) document the group's evolution. Its music frequently used original compositions, ambiguous tonal centers and collective improvisation rather than conventional solo-and-accompaniment structures.

The quintet became one of the most influential small ensembles in modern jazz. Hancock, Shorter, Carter and Williams went on to major careers of their own, while Davis continued to push the boundaries of ensemble performance.

Electric Jazz and Bitches Brew, 1968–1975

In the late 1960s, Davis incorporated electric keyboards, amplified instruments, rock rhythms and funk into his music. Albums such as In a Silent Way (1969) introduced an expansive electric sound built from sustained textures, repeated patterns and studio editing.

Bitches Brew, released in 1970, marked a decisive turn toward electric jazz fusion. Its large ensemble included multiple keyboardists, guitarists and percussionists, with the music shaped through layered performances and extensive studio editing. The album challenged established ideas about jazz composition, improvisation and recording.

Davis continued this direction with albums including A Tribute to Jack Johnson (1971), Live-Evil (1971), On the Corner (1972) and Get Up with It (1974). These recordings drew on funk, rock, African musical influences and experimental studio techniques. His concerts increasingly emphasized sustained grooves, amplified sound and fluid transitions between compositions.

Withdrawal from Performing, 1975–1980

By the mid-1970s, Davis was suffering from serious physical and personal difficulties, including hip problems, pain and substance use. He withdrew from regular public performance in 1975 and spent several years largely outside the music industry.

Although he did not release a steady stream of new recordings during this period, his earlier work continued to influence jazz, rock and emerging electronic music. His absence also heightened anticipation for his eventual return.

Return and the 1980s, 1981–1991

Davis returned to recording and touring in 1981. His new groups brought together younger musicians and incorporated contemporary funk, pop, rock and electronic sounds. Albums such as The Man with the Horn (1981), Star People (1983), Decoy (1984) and You're Under Arrest (1985) reflected his continuing interest in current musical styles.

His 1980s work also included reinterpretations of popular songs. The album Tutu (1986), produced with a major contribution from Marcus Miller, used synthesizers, programmed rhythms and layered production to create a contemporary sound. Amandla (1989) continued this approach.

Davis remained an internationally prominent performer and collaborated with musicians across genres. In 1986, he received a Grammy Award for the instrumental jazz album Tutu. His late-career output demonstrated that he remained willing to change his sound rather than recreate his earlier successes.

Death and Legacy

Miles Davis died on September 28, 1991, in Santa Monica, California, at the age of 65. His death followed a period of serious illness.

Across five decades, Davis helped shape cool jazz, hard bop, modal jazz and electric jazz fusion. His recordings established new approaches to improvisation, ensemble interaction, orchestration and studio production.

His influence also extended through the musicians he hired and encouraged. Several members of his groups became major bandleaders and composers, carrying ideas developed in his ensembles into new musical directions.

Davis is remembered both for specific landmark recordings and for his broader role in repeatedly redefining jazz. His career demonstrated that a musician could build a lasting legacy not by preserving a signature formula, but by continually changing it.
